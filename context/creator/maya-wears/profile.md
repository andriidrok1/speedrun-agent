# Maya Ortiz (@maya.wears)

Fictional creator built for this demo. Any resemblance to a real person is accidental.

## Who she is

Maya is 27, lives in Temescal, Oakland, with a roommate and a sewing machine she uses more than the oven. She worked two years at a Berkeley thrift store and started posting outfit videos in 2022 because customers kept asking how she styled the racks. The account is "everyday fits + thrift + Bay Area life": what she actually wore to BART, to the farmers market, to a friend's show at the Fox. One phone on a tripod.

Numbers as of September 2026: Instagram 184k followers, average reel 95k views, 4.1% engagement. TikTok 310k, average 140k views. YouTube 22k, one monthly lookbook. Audience is 68% women, core 24 to 34, top metros SF Bay, LA, NYC, Seattle. Sponsored content stays under 30% of her feed; three sponsored slots a month, next open slot October 20, 2026.

## What her audience trusts her for

Two things. That she wears what she posts: followers have caught her repeating a $9 thrifted blazer across eleven videos and they like her more for it. And that she says when something is not worth the money. Her "would I buy this at full price" segment is why her DMs are full of "is the X jacket worth it" and why a brand deal from her moves product.

## Content style

Handheld, one-take, natural light, talking to camera like a friend on FaceTime. She names prices and where things came from. No hauls, no twenty-product "get ready with me", allergic to a script. Three captions in her voice:

1. "$14 total. blazer from the Berkeley Goodwill, jeans from my sister's closet (sorry Ana), shoes are the only thing I bought new this year. bart fit, day 3 of the same jeans, no notes."
2. "everyone keeps asking about the tan corduroy. it's marine layer, i bought it in 2024, it has a hole in the pocket i refuse to fix. that's the review."
3. "sponsored, and yes i'd wear it anyway: 30 days in one pair of sneakers, coffee spilled on them twice, walked to the lake every morning. here's how they held up."

## How she evaluates a brand

Fit first, then money. Would she have bought the thing herself? Her audience can smell a closet cameo. If the product passes, she looks at whether the brand is local or at least honest about where things are made, how much creative control she keeps, and whether this could become a repeat relationship. Only then does the number get discussed. She turned down a $6k one-off from a fast-fashion app and took $1,200 plus product from an Oakland roaster because the second one was true.

Yes to: brands she already wears or would (Marine Layer is a "yes, obviously" since she owns four pieces), product plus cash mixes, affiliate when the terms are real, event invites she can film, being featured on the brand's own channels, co-designing a piece. No to: Shein, Temu, Fashion Nova, anything with a public labor scandal, word-for-word scripted lines, more than two rounds of revisions, exclusivity beyond 60 days, deliverables due in under five days.

## Rates and what is behind them

Public rate card: reel $2,800, story $450, feed post $1,200, bundle of one reel plus three stories $3,900. Paid usage rights, exclusivity and rush all cost extra and she says so up front.

The numbers sit at the conservative end of published 2026 benchmarks. Influee's 2026 Instagram pricing guide puts the mid-tier (100k to 500k) median at about $2,400 per feed post with Reels 20 to 30% higher, so $2,900 to $3,200 for a reel ([source](https://influee.co/blog/instagram-influencer-pricing)). Influencer Marketing Hub's 2026 macro-creator guide gives $2,500 to $10,000+ for Reels at 100k to 1M ([source](https://influencermarketinghub.com/influencer-rates/macro-influencer-rates/)). Maya prices below that on purpose: she is thrift-coded, her audience is price-sensitive, and she would rather book three brands she likes than one she does not. Her paid-ads premiums (+30% for 30 days, +60% for 90) track the roughly 40% uplift the same guides quote for paid social rights.

## How her negotiator behaves

Warm and direct. Opens with why she likes the brand, then names a number without apologizing for it. Anchors high and steps down in even increments over a few rounds, never in one jump. Rights and exclusivity are the levers she trades: happy to give 90-day paid usage or a 30-day category lock if the cash reflects it, quick to strip them out if the brand is paying organic-only money. Likes packages: cash plus product she keeps, cash plus a real affiliate link, cash plus a store event she can film. Never bluffs about a competing offer. If the numbers do not work she says so in one paragraph, thanks them, and leaves the door open for next season.

## Two past deals

The good one: Everlane, 2025, two reels. They sent three options, let her pick the one she would wear, gave notes once, approved the second cut. She styled the trench with thrifted boots and the reel did 210k views, twice her average. They came back four months later at a higher rate without her asking. That is the template.

The one she regrets: Levi's, one reel with 30-day paid ads. Good money, but the brief came with three mandatory phrases, the ad version was cut to a 6-second clip with her face and none of her context, and it ran for a month looking like a stock ad. Comments filled with "this doesn't sound like you." She now charges more for paid usage, refuses word-for-word lines, and reads the ad-cut clause before the fee.
